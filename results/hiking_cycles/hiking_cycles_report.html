Interim pauses versus terminal pauses

The rule buys duration in any hold after a hike. Looking back, a pause either ended with another hike (interim) or with the cycle turning to easing or ending (terminal). Real time can't tell them apart.

14 of 20completed pauses were interim, followed by another hike. Mean -0.21% vs bills; 6 of 14 positive.
6 of 20were terminal. Mean +8.1% vs bills; 6 of 6 positive. They lasted 199 sessions on average against 49 for interim.
1 undeterminedThe current pause (cycle 7, 7 sessions old). Nothing yet says which type it is.

The edge comes from a few long terminal pauses (1997–98, 2000–01, 2023–24, 2019, 1995). Median pause return is +0.6%.

What it adds up to

Staying out of duration while the Fed hikes was right on balance: 13 of 21 hiking spells lost to bills, mean -1.1%, and the 2004–06 and 2022–23 spells lost 3.2% and 11.4%. The cost was the pauses. The interim ones lose or break even, while the terminal ones earn most of the rule's edge. Cycle-equal-weight 8Y 3-month excess is +1.27% in pauses (95% interval +0.55% to +2.04%) and +0.33% during hiking (−0.87% to +1.66%), over six complete cycles.

Read this with care

  • Only six complete cycles, so intervals are cycle-bootstrap and wide. 2015 onward are reserved cycles, not clean out-of-sample.
  • Splitting pauses into interim and terminal uses what happened afterwards. It is a description, not a signal the rule could use.
  • The current pause has the usual ambiguity. Two dissenters wanted a hike and the 2026 cycle has one hike, so an interim pause is a live possibility. The history can't say how likely, because no earlier cycle paused after a single hike except 1997 (terminal).
  • Returns are model-estimated par-bond returns, not tradable index returns. Historical events use date-only timing for 152 of 271 meetings, entered from the next day.
  • The inflation card uses an interpolated October 2025 base stored in macrodb, so the October YoY states are provisional.
